01 · Too many signals

Most of what a house measures is noise.

Every minute the thermostat reads twelve sensors. The sun on the window, the oven and the front door move the room temperature. The Wi-Fi signal, the doorbell battery and the clock do not.

Nobody tells the learner which is which. It has to work that out from the readings alone.

    02 · One speed is a bad compromise

    A single learning speed is either late or nervous.

    A plain learner changes its mind at one fixed speed, the same for every sensor. Set it low and it takes ages to notice the oven is on. Set it high and every flicker of the Wi-Fi signal shakes the prediction.

    What we want: fast for the oven, still for the clock. No single speed does both.

    curves are sketches, not measurements

    03 · A speed for every sensor

    IDBD gives each sensor its own speed, and keeps tuning it.

    Richard Sutton called it Incremental Delta-Bar-Delta in 1992.

    It watches how its own corrections behave, one sensor at a time.

    A sensor's corrections keep pointing the same way: its speed goes up.

    Its corrections cancel each other out: its speed goes down.

    04 · The house never sits still

    What matters keeps changing. Nobody announces it.

    Clouds cross the sun. Someone opens the kitchen door. The front door swings open and shut. After each change, the corrections for that sensor all point the same way until the learner catches up, so IDBD keeps those three sensors fast. The noise sensors' corrections keep cancelling out, so they stay slow.
