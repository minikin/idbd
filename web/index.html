IDBD · Sutton, 1992

A learner that works out what to listen to.

IDBD is a rule for learning from a stream of data. It keeps a separate learning speed for every input and tunes those speeds as it goes, so it reacts fast to what matters and tunes out what doesn't.

    What IDBD does

    Guess and check. Decide how hard to correct, then correct.

    A home assistant wants to predict the power meter. Every minute it reads its twelve sensors, guesses the power draw, sees what the meter really says and corrects itself. Most learning programs work this way. IDBD adds one thing: every sensor gets its own learning speed, and IDBD tunes those speeds before every correction. Below, the four steps run on the same home, one reading at a time.

    1. 1

      Guess

      Multiply each reading by the trust in its sensor and add them up.

    2. 2

      Check

      Read the meter. The difference from the guess is the miss.

    3. 3

      Tune the speeds

      Compare each sensor's correction with its recent ones. If they keep pushing the same way, speed that sensor up. If they keep cancelling out, slow it down. This step is IDBD.

    4. 4

      Correct

      Move each trust to shrink the miss, as far as that sensor's speed allows, and remember the move so the next reading can be compared with it.

    01 · Too many signals

    Two thirds of what this home measures never touches the bill.

    The assistant reads twelve sensors every minute: thermometers, the Wi-Fi router, the doorbell, the front door, the air, even the plant's soil. Four of them add to the power bill, each during its own hours of the day. The other eight never do.

    Nobody tells the learner which four, or when. It has to work that out from the readings alone. And the sensors read on different scales: some swing four times wider than others.

      02 · One speed is a bad compromise

      A single learning speed is either late or nervous.

      A plain learner corrects every sensor with one fixed step size. In this home the sensors read on different scales, so one step size is slow on sensors with small readings and jumpy on sensors with large ones. Here are two of them, unnamed so the game stays a game.

      What we want: quick on the small readings, still on the large ones. One step size cannot do both. IDBD keeps one per sensor.

        What you just watched

        IDBD's bars show what it believes each sensor adds to the meter at each hour. They run a little late: a belief takes time to grow after a sensor starts to matter and to fade after it stops.

        Every sensor here reads on its own scale, the way a barometer and an oven thermometer do. One fixed learning speed cannot suit them all, and that is where IDBD's edge comes from. Rescale every reading to the same spread first, as the idbd README recommends, and a well-tuned fixed speed does as well as IDBD: over ten simulated runs its bars scored 89% to IDBD's 85%, and its error was only a little higher.

        This demonstrates how IDBD finds what matters. Family Assistant only predicted the meter from the sensors. It did not switch anything on or off. Method and results over ten simulated runs.

        05 · What IDBD learned

        After three days, IDBD listens closely to four sensors and lets eight go quiet.

        Nobody told it which sensors matter. The corrections for the water heater, the wall, the oven and the Wi-Fi data use kept agreeing, and the others kept cancelling out.

        Speeds rise a little in the hours a sensor is busy, but not enough to tell those hours apart. The game's hour bars come from the trust, not the speed.

        Richard Sutton called the rule Incremental Delta-Bar-Delta in 1992.
