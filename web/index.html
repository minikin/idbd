IDBD · Sutton, 1992

A learner that works out what to listen to.

IDBD is a rule for learning from a stream of data. It keeps a separate learning speed for every input and tunes those speeds as it goes, so it reacts fast to what matters and tunes out what doesn't.

    What IDBD does

    Guess and check. Decide how hard to correct, then correct.

    A thermostat wants to predict how the room temperature will change. Every minute it reads its sensors, makes a guess, sees what really happened and corrects itself. Most learning programs work this way. IDBD adds one thing: for every sensor it keeps its own learning speed, and it adjusts those speeds before every correction.

    1. 1

      Guess

      Multiply each sensor's reading by how much it trusts that sensor, and add them up.

    2. 2

      Check

      See the real temperature change. The difference is the miss.

    3. 3

      Tune the speeds

      If a sensor's corrections keep pushing the same way, speed it up. If they keep cancelling out, slow it down. This step is IDBD.

    4. 4

      Correct

      Change each trust a little to shrink the miss, as far as that sensor's new speed allows. Remember the correction, so the next reading can be compared with it.

    01 · Too many signals

    Half of what this house measures is noise.

    The thermostat reads six sensors every minute. The sun on the window and the oven warm the room. An open front door lets heat out, so it cools the room. The Wi-Fi signal, the doorbell battery and the water heater, which sits in a closed boiler room and heats water, not this room, do nothing to the temperature.

    Nobody tells the learner which is which. It has to work that out from the readings alone.

      02 · One speed is a bad compromise

      A single learning speed is either late or nervous.

      A plain learner corrects every sensor at one fixed speed. Set it low and it takes ages to notice the oven is on. Set it high and every flicker of the Wi-Fi signal shakes the guess.

      What we want: fast for the oven, still for the Wi-Fi. No single speed does both.

      04 · A speed for every sensor

      After a while, IDBD listens to three sensors and tunes out three.

      Nobody told it which sensors matter. The speeds for the sun, the oven and the front door grew because their corrections kept agreeing. The others shrank because theirs kept cancelling out.

      Richard Sutton called the rule Incremental Delta-Bar-Delta in 1992.

      05 · The house never sits still

      What matters keeps changing. Nobody announces it.

      Clouds cross the sun. The oven goes on and off. The front door swings open and shut. After each change, that sensor's corrections all push the same way for a while, so IDBD keeps those three sensors fast and catches up quickly.

      How to play

      1. You guess too. Mark each sensor: does it warm the room, cool it, or not matter? Every reading, your guess adds up the usual effect of the sensors you marked.
      2. Highest accuracy wins. 100% is as close as anyone can get: the readings are noisy, so even a perfect guess misses a little. 0% is no better than always guessing "the temperature won't change". A round is 600 readings, one minute.
